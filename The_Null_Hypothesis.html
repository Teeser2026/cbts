The Null Hypothesis

H₀: It is possible to exist outside the surveillance architecture

This document tries to reject it. Every conceivable countermeasure, tested against capabilities that are deployed, documented or for sale. If one survives, the hypothesis stands. If none does, it falls.

Outside means beyond its reach. Not beyond its gaze: being ignored doesn’t count.

A null hypothesis is never proven. It is only rejected, or not. Hold that thought until the conclusion.

Eight hundred years of promises.

Promises based on the wisdom of philosophers, prophets, revolutionaries and lawmakers. From Runnymede to Rome. From the King James Bible to the United Nations.

Inalienable rights. Rights that don’t come from the state. Rights that the state can’t take away.

In theory.

This document tests whether any of them still function.

Privacy is what human rights law calls a gateway right — the right that makes the others possible. Freedom of expression, freedom of assembly, freedom from discrimination — each assumes you can think, move, and associate without being watched. Remove privacy and the rest don’t need to be repealed. They just stop working.

Three companion documents supply the evidence base. Each describes a layer of the architecture. Each renders a specific promise technically unenforceable.

The Surveillance Architecture catalogues the observation layer — what is deployed, how it arrived, how it integrates. Thirteen police forces now run live facial recognition. The Met alone scanned around four million faces in 2025. One afternoon at Oxford Circus: more than 50,000 in four and a half hours. Behind the cameras, over 150 million passport and immigration photos have been made searchable by police facial recognition. Nobody told Parliament. Your phone’s advertising data is sold to law enforcement without a warrant. Your body — its gait, its heartbeat, its effect on WiFi signals — is readable through walls, without a device, without your knowledge. The right to be secure in your person assumes you can’t be searched without contact. That assumption is now technically false.

“The advancement of science and the diffusion of information [is] the best aliment to true liberty.”

— James Madison

The Science of Surveillance catalogues the science layer — the published research, ratified standards, and funded programmes behind both current deployment and emerging capability. Your body broadcasts dozens of unique biometric identifiers. The Science of Surveillance catalogues 46 of them. 19 of those are structural — they cannot be altered. The rest reassert themselves the moment you stop trying to suppress them. The science described in this companion document is the advancement of surveillance — published, peer-reviewed, and funded. The antithesis of liberty. The right to privacy assumes a boundary between the person and the observer. Your body is the broadcast.

“Those who deny freedom to others, deserve it not for themselves.”

— Abraham Lincoln

The Interoperable State catalogues the identity layer — how digital identity systems are being built, legislated, and connected. GOV.UK One Login. Age checks built into your phone’s operating system, which Apple wasn’t required to add and the regulator called a win. Mandatory identity verification for six to seven million company directors, with criminal sanctions behind it. However they verify, the check ties a passport or licence the state already holds their face against to a single personal code, needed for every company they run. The state denies freedom to its citizens while classifying its own meetings, redacting its own contracts, and refusing its own FOI requests. It claims privacy for its deliberations while removing it from everyone else. The right to correspondence without interference assumes the system needs a warrant to know who you are. Your phone now asks you to prove you’re an adult before it treats you as one. No law required it. The government nudged, the regulator collaborated, Apple complied, and the regulator winked a thank you.

The architecture doesn’t need to repeal your rights. It doesn’t need to amend them, debate them, or put them to a vote. It just needs to make them technically unenforceable while leaving the text on the page. The law still says what it says. The infrastructure doesn’t care.

And the deepest layer isn’t coercion. It’s desire. The most intimate surveillance — continuous heart rate, sleep patterns, location, movement, your intimate words — comes from the device you love, the one you chose, the one you wear to bed, the one you carry in your pocket.

The architecture’s most effective mechanism is not the camera on the lamppost.

It’s the watch on your wrist. It’s the phone in your hand.

The test

You are the most technically literate, privacy-conscious person alive. Here is your best plan — and why it fails against capabilities that are publicly documented, commercially available, or proven by leaked operational records.

Your moveWhy it fails
Use encryptionThe maths is sound. The question is who chooses it. In June 2026 the US moved to one approved list of post-quantum algorithms, defined deviation as a reportable flaw, and set out to inventory the cryptography in every component. The same institutions once approved an algorithm with a suspected back door. (See Appendix: The engine and the lock.)
Use a zero-knowledge proofThe maths works: you can prove you’re over 18 without revealing who you are. But a proof is only useful if someone accepts it, and nobody is required to. The government’s own consultation asked a different question: whether adults should complete age checks more often.
Use a VPNHides your IP from the destination. Not from the VPN provider. Not from your device fingerprint, typing cadence, or behavioural biometrics. And since March 2026, UK iPhones enforce age restrictions inside the operating system itself. The filter lives on the phone, not the network. The tunnel doesn’t touch it.
Use SignalThe encryption protects the pipe. Nobody is attacking the pipe. Cellebrite extracts from the endpoints. Pegasus reads the screen buffer before encryption applies.
Use TorExit nodes can be watched. Anyone can run one, including the people you’re hiding from. Traffic analysis correlates entry and exit timing. Your ISP sees you connecting to Tor. Using Tor is itself a flag. Silk Road, AlphaBay, Hansa, Hydra: all taken down. Not by breaking the encryption. Mostly by exploiting the humans around it.
Use an accountant, not One LoginYou can, on paper. In practice, many small firms won’t offer the service: too complex, too much risk. Either way, Companies House still ties the passport the state already holds your face against to a personal code you need for every company you run. File anything yourself and you’re asked to connect to One Login anyway.
Use the legal systemThe system wrote the laws. FOI requests are refused under national security exemptions upheld by tribunals. The Investigatory Powers Act legalised bulk collection. The Data Use and Access Act enables interoperability. Meanwhile, six to seven million directors and PSCs must verify their identity by 18 November 2026. Non-compliance is a criminal offence. Want to bid for a public contract? You need a One Login account for that too. Legal challenges take years. The architecture is deployed in months. By the time the court rules, the capability has been normalised.
Use GrapheneOSMost banking apps still run. Some refuse, some stop and start again, some need workarounds. Tap-to-pay doesn’t. And the dial isn’t yours: Google is slowly tightening the checks apps rely on, and apps adopt them faster than they make exceptions. The phone works. Whether your bank does is decided somewhere else, one update at a time.
Use a burnerUnique IMEI. The shop has facial recognition. The SIM requires registration. Your voice is a biometric. Cellebrite extracts from burners.
Drive instead of flyBritain didn’t need to buy Flock. It built its own. Around 13,000 police cameras feed over 60 million number-plate reads a day into a single national database, searchable by every force and kept for a year. In America, Flock Safety’s 120,000 cameras record 20 billion scans a month, and a Washington Post count found at least 26 cases of officers using plate readers to track wives, girlfriends and exes. The difference isn’t the scale. It’s who owns the database.
Use cryptoThe blockchain is public. Every transaction is permanent. Chain analysis firms — contracted by law enforcement — trace through mixing with increasing accuracy. Converting crypto to goods requires meeting someone who carries a phone.
Go cash onlyShops stop accepting it. ATMs disappearing. Cash marks you as anomalous. The person you’re buying from carries a phone. The Bank of England promises a digital pound would never be programmable by the state. But if programmable money arrives, by CBDC, stablecoin or some other route, held on the same phone, behind the same verified identity as your driving licence, cash stops being a viable alternative. It becomes an archaeological artefact.
Ditch the smartphoneYour absence is the signal. No IMSI = anomaly. Every person you interact with carries a phone that logs your proximity via Bluetooth and Wi-Fi probe requests. You are visible in other people’s data. Penlink’s Webloc draws on advertising data from up to 500 million devices, used by police and intelligence agencies from the US to Hungary to El Salvador. No warrant. No carrier. No phone tower. The advertising layer is the surveillance layer.
Use mesh networkingRF emissions detectable and locatable via direction-finding. Hardware manufactured by a company with records. Firmware fingerprint-able. Each node is a point on a map. The network is tolerated because it threatens nothing.
Build a resistance networkJoining means being a member. Being a member means being on a list. Being on a list is the thing you’re avoiding. The network is the surveillance architecture you’re refusing — just run by people you agree with instead of people you don’t.
Change your appearanceGait analysis identifies how you walk, from behind, at distance, through disguise. Ear shape, vein patterns, hand geometry. Voiceprint identifies you on any call. Your heartbeat has a shape personal enough to identify you, and a radar can read it without touching you. In one study it picked out the right person nineteen times in twenty. Thirteen people, in a lab, sitting still. So far. Your body broadcasts dozens of unique biometric identifiers. No disguise addresses more than a handful.
Flee the countryAirports are the most locked-down biometric infrastructure in existence. Passport facial recognition, e-gates, boarding pass identity verification. Private aviation requires filed flight plans. Destination jurisdictions have their own surveillance architecture. You’re not escaping the system. You’re moving to a different node in the same network. The EU requires all 27 member states to offer a digital identity wallet by the end of 2026. India’s Aadhaar has enrolled over 1.3 billion. The architecture is global.
Never go onlineYou exist in other people’s data. Photos searchable via facial recognition. Every person you meet whose phone has location services creates a proximity record. Electoral roll, property records, vehicle registration, medical history — all extractable, all cross-referenceable. You went dark. Your file didn’t.
Become a hermitNo devices. No society. No income. Your property records still exist. Commercial satellites see 30cm, and a licence to sell 10cm imagery has been granted, and the satellite flew. WiFi sensing became an official IEEE standard in September 2025, and research systems have already read breathing, heartbeats and movement through walls. If there is WiFi near you, it can be a sensor.

And listening doesn’t need a microphone. In 2014, researchers recovered speech from silent video of a crisp packet. By 2024, an off-the-shelf radar could pick up a phone call from the earpiece’s vibrations and transcribe it. By 2025, radar was reading lips through a wall, and a single off-the-shelf radar, with AI filling in what it couldn’t hear, was rebuilding whole sentences from any speaker. Its authors called it particularly suitable for eavesdropping. By 2026, WiFi was reading silent lips nearly as well as a camera. So far, each one needs a lab, a list or a willing subject. So far.

One man came close. In 1986, aged 20, Christopher Knight walked into the Maine woods and stayed for 27 years. No devices. No campfires. No conversation. “To the rest of the world, I ceased to exist.” But he still had to eat. He broke into holiday cabins more than a thousand times. For twenty-five years, sheriffs, state police and game wardens hunted the burglar, and failed. In 2013, a game warden hid one motion sensor in a camp kitchen. It took one. Today, the cabins would be watching him themselves. He was never outside. He was living off its edges, and the edges are where the sensors go.

The conclusion

Which brings us to what feels like an exit, but isn’t. You’re not hiding, you’re being ignored. The system can collect everything. It doesn’t need to act on everything. Not yet. And soon it won’t have to choose. You avoid the feeling of being surveilled not because you are invisible or untracked, but because you’re boring. But they’re building something that finds dull interesting.

One could argue that every move in the table has an exit.

Look at what each exit costs. No banking app. No government services. No public contracts. No company, unless someone else files for it. No work, if your employer checks the wrong way. No phone that works like everyone else’s. The exits aren’t sealed. They’re priced. And the price rises every year, quietly, one app update and one compliance deadline at a time.

So is it possible to exist outside this architecture? Even if it means abandoning any worthwhile definition of “existence”? Living as a hermit. Off-grid. No devices. No society. No income. You think even that would make you untraceable?

No. It is only possible to be ignored by it. To be irrelevant, which is not the same as being outside. Right up to the point when you’re deemed relevant.

The null hypothesis must be rejected. Not because the exits don’t exist, but because none of them leads anywhere you’d want to live. There is no countermeasure that survives contact with the known, documented, publicly available surveillance architecture. Every test fails.

But I’ve done nothing wrong. I’ve got nothing to hide. I’ve got nothing to worry about.

That argument is based on several assumptions. That the current definition of “wrong” is widely accepted. That it is reasonable. That it is enforced fairly. That the people defining “wrong” are trustworthy. That they’ll always be trustworthy. And that an architecture built for one purpose will never be repurposed for another.

History says none of those are safe bets.

The question is not whether you have something to hide. The question is whether you trust every future government, in every future crisis, with a surveillance architecture this complete, this integrated, this inescapable — forever — with no off switch you control.

The architecture doesn’t need to be malicious today. It needs to be capable. And it is.

So the last question this document can ask is not a technical one. It’s a personal one. What do you do when the system you live inside has made the rights you were promised — that are yours by right — technically unenforceable? And the tools you’d need to resist it are the very tools it built?

Some people comply and don’t think about it. Most people.

Some people comply and think about it constantly. That’s worse.

Some people refuse. Not because it will achieve something. Because something in them won’t let them not.

People who refuse alone get picked off in their thousands. Millions refusing together is a different beast. The architecture’s real defence isn’t the camera. It’s the fact that each of us thinks we’re the only one who noticed.

This document can’t tell you which one to be. It can only show you the architecture, and what every exit costs.

You have the liberty to do with that what you will. For now.

A / Appendix

The engine and the lock

Part 1 · The lock (documented)

This is the section most people will skip because it sounds like cryptography, which sounds like maths, which sounds like someone else’s problem. It is not someone else’s problem. It is the floor beneath every other problem in this document.

Every test above — the VPN, Signal, Tor, the cold wallet, the mesh network — assumes that the encryption protecting your communication is yours. That the mathematics securing your transaction is neutral. That the cryptographic standard your device runs was designed to protect you from the system, or at minimum, wasn’t designed to protect the system from you.

That assumption is wrong.

On 22 June 2026 the President signed two executive orders on the same day, with the same pen. The first, Executive Order 14412, moves federal systems to the post-quantum algorithms approved by the National Institute of Standards and Technology, and reaches contractors through procurement rules. High-value systems must switch their key exchange by the end of 2030 and their signatures by the end of 2031. The second launches a national effort to build a quantum computer, and deliver it to the Department of Energy.

The engine and the lock. Same afternoon.

Three provisions tell the story.

The Secretary of State, working with the Pentagon and the Director of National Intelligence, is to engage foreign governments and industry in key countries and encourage them onto the NIST algorithms. The standard isn’t only for home. It’s for export.

Contractors must comply with NIST’s standards by the end of 2030, and their vulnerability-disclosure programmes must test for the use of non-approved algorithms, listed alongside having no encryption at all. Not banned. Defined as broken.

And a cryptographic bill of materials: a machine-readable list of the cryptography inside each piece of hardware and software, designed for automated assessment.

One approved list. Mandatory through procurement. Deviation reported as a flaw. Every component inventoried by machine. Exported to allies.

To be fair to the lock: NIST didn’t design it. The new algorithms came out of an open, eight-year international competition, written by academic cryptographers and attacked in public by anyone who cared to try. If there is a hidden flaw, the whole world had the chance to find it.

But there is a precedent. In 2006 NIST approved a random number generator called Dual_EC_DRBG, designed by the NSA. Within a year, two Microsoft researchers showed it could hide a back door. In 2013 the Snowden documents pointed straight at it, and Reuters reported that the NSA had paid RSA $10 million to make it the default in a widely used security library. RSA denies knowing. NIST withdrew it in 2014.

And the older locks, the ones cryptographers had eyed for twenty years? In October 2026, Anthropic’s researchers used Claude to trace where the seeds of NIST’s elliptic curves came from: an ordinary 1990s random-number generator, seeded with timestamps. P-256 was born at 01:52 on 16 August 1997. Every seed explained but one. B-163’s is still a mystery. Not rigged, as far as anyone can tell. Just unexplained.

So the risk isn’t that today’s algorithms are broken. It’s that the same institutions now decide which locks count as approved, keep a list of where every one is fitted, and have already been caught leaving a key under the mat.

Not every lock is theirs, yet. Bitcoin’s signatures use a curve NIST never picked. Signal wraps its post-quantum layer around an older, independent one, so one broken lock isn’t enough. Those are exactly the systems a single approved standard, enforced through procurement, will put under pressure.

The quantum threat itself is real. The mathematics that would let a large quantum computer break today’s encryption has been known since 1994. That is what makes it the perfect justification. Nobody has to invent the emergency. They only have to decide who answers it, and with whose lock.

Part 2 · The engine (a bet)

Everything above describes a system that can see, hear, inventory, and authenticate. What it has not been able to do — for ninety years, since the first technocratic planners proposed running a society as an engineering problem — is compute at the scale required to act on what it sees. The surveillance captures the data. Palantir cross-references it. But optimising against it in real time — directing resource flows, modelling an entire economy, making the plan work faster than the reality changes — exceeded what any computer could do.

Markets solved this by distributing the computation across millions of actors and prices. Central planning tried to consolidate it and failed. The mainframe couldn’t do it. The internet couldn’t do it. The AI data centres couldn’t do it.

That is the gap the quantum programme puts within reach, if the optimism is right. The executive order directs a quantum computer to be delivered to a Department of Energy laboratory. The Department has already folded it into the Genesis Mission: one ecosystem of supercomputers, AI and quantum machines, under one roof. The brain and its engine arrive at the same address.

A quantum computer is not a faster classical computer. Whether it helps with planning at the scale of an economy is unproven. The serious bets are chemistry, materials, and breaking today’s encryption. But the Energy Secretary says the error-corrected version arrives within this administration, and the address is already chosen.

We don’t know if they’ll get there. Quantum at that scale is a bet. But they’ve bet the country’s technology policy on it, and they’ve structured the entire executive-order architecture around the assumption that it will work.

They didn’t need to build a new system. They needed to make the old one fail. The new one was already installed.
